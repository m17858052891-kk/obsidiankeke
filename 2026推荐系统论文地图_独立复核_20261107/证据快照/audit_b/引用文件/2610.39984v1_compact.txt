## R2QRec: Learning Recommendation Queries from Retrieval Feedback
 Weixin Chen1, Shuchang Liu1 $\dagger$ , Hailan Yang1, Yuhan Zhao1,2, Li Kang2, Xuefeng Zhang1, Chenghao Zhang1, Xinyuan Wang1, Hanchen Yang1, Yinghao Wu1, Hua Huang1, Yandong Bai1, Fan Yang1, Xiang Li1, Chunjie Chen1, Li Chen2 Affiliation: 1Kuaishou Technology  2Hong Kong Baptist University © none 
## Abstract.
Query-based recommendation separates expressing user needs from locating matching items. Its central learning problem is how to turn item interactions into effective retrieval requests when interaction logs do not annotate queries. We present R2QRec, which uses a fixed retrieval system to construct query supervision and evaluate recommendation outcomes. Retrieval verification first selects generated expressions that recover observed items as supervised fine tuning targets, allowing retrieval behavior to determine the target wording. Policy refinement then evaluates generated query sets by the observed item’s rank in their fused recommendation list. Log-rank feedback distinguishes outcomes beyond the recommendation cutoff, while a KL penalty constrains policy change relative to the supervised reference. The stages have distinct roles: verification identifies expressions to imitate, and policy refinement improves the combined ranking produced by newly generated queries. Only the query generator is trained; the retriever and item index remain fixed, and no query annotations or learned reward model are required. Across three public datasets, R2QRec achieves state-of-the-art overall recommendation performance among the evaluated methods. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed design and its generalization across generators and domains.

## 1. Introduction
Recommendation on Web platforms requires finding items that satisfy a user’s needs, even when those needs do not specify a concrete retrieval request. Interaction histories and other available user context provide evidence of preferences, inducing several plausible directions for the next recommendation. Existing approaches express these preferences through discriminative item scores (Kang and McAuley, 2018; He et al., 2020) or distributions on discrete item identifiers (Rajput et al., 2023; Deng et al., 2025).
Query-based recommendation makes this intermediate preference expression explicit in natural language, then uses a retriever to find matching items across the catalog (Li et al., 2023b; Han et al., 2025). A single user can have multiple interests (Li et al., 2019). For example, a reading history containing Thinking, Fast and Slow, The Power of Habit, and Outliers may suggest interests in cognitive science, behavioral change, or human achievement. Large language models (LLMs) can draw on semantic knowledge to turn these plausible interests into separate retrieval requests, with prior work demonstrating the value of multiple queries for recommendation (Li et al., 2023b). This provides an open-ended language representation of preferences without requiring the generator to select an item code or enumerate a candidate list.
Title-supervised and prompting-based recommenders provide two ways to obtain retrieval text. GPT4Rec trains its generator to predict target item titles before using the generated text for retrieval (Li et al., 2023b); BIGRec also uses target item text for supervised adaptation (Bao et al., 2025). QueRec (Han et al., 2025) generates personalized queries through prompting without LLM fine-tuning. We study a complementary learning formulation: next-item interactions supply query supervision and evaluate the recommendation outcomes of generated queries (Figure 1).
An observed interaction identifies an item to recover, but does not prescribe a unique query to reproduce. Several expressions may retrieve the same item, while the interaction log does not annotate which expression to imitate. We address this supervision problem through item-verified supervision: generated expressions that retrieve the observed item successfully become training targets. The item label thereby specifies an admissibility criterion for queries without fixing their wording.
Admissible individual expressions and useful query combinations pose different ranking problems. Even the complete vector of target ranks across queries does not determine the fused recommendation outcome: the positions of competing items across the lists also matter. Our analysis formalizes this distinction, motivating fusion-aware refinement of generated sets using the observed item’s rank after reciprocal rank fusion. Log-rank feedback then supplies relative comparisons among sets for the same context (Shao et al., 2024). Its cutoff-risk decomposition explains how it emphasizes improvements near the head while retaining feedback beyond a fixed recommendation cutoff.
These choices define R2QRec, a compact framework linking item-level supervision to recommendation-level evaluation. Item-verified supervision constructs the expressions to imitate; fusion-aware refinement evaluates newly generated combinations; the supervised reference constrains policy change through a KL penalty. The same frozen retrieval interface supplies both supervision and outcome evaluation, while only the query generator is updated. This makes recommendation queries a learned interface between user context and item retrieval.
At inference, the generator expresses user preferences as several queries, the retriever executes them, and reciprocal rank fusion combines the resulting item lists (Cormack et al., 2009). The item index and retrieval model remain fixed, and the learning procedure requires neither annotated queries nor a separate learned reward model.
We evaluate R2QRec on three public recommendation datasets and observe state-of-the-art overall recommendation performance among the evaluated methods. Controlled MovieTV comparisons trace title supervision and retrieval-verified supervision through SFT and subsequent policy refinement. These comparisons connect the design choices to their effect on recommendation quality; further experiments examine multiple queries, model choices, and domain transfer.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• 
Item-supervised query learning. We formulate query supervision through the retrieval admissibility of generated expressions, allowing an observed interaction to supervise multiple phrasings without query annotations.
• 
Fusion-aware refinement and analysis. We show that individual target ranks do not determine fused recommendation utility, motivating feedback on query combinations. A cutoff-risk decomposition characterizes the depth weighting of the log-rank objective.
• 
Controlled evidence for the learning design. Experiments distinguish the effect of supervision source from subsequent policy refinement and evaluate recommendation quality across three domains.

## 2. Preliminaries
Let $\mathcal{U}$ and $\mathcal{I}$ denote the user and item sets. For user $u$ , let $H_{u}$ denote the chronological interaction history. The user context $s_{u}$ represents the user’s recommendation needs, primarily through their interaction history. Given $s_{u}$ , the system retrieves and ranks items from $\mathcal{I}\setminus H_{u}$ as recommendations, where the held-out next item $i^{\ast}$ serves as the target.
Interaction logs provide item targets without annotated queries. For a chronological sequence $(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n})$ , the final interaction $i_{n}$ is reserved for evaluation. An earlier interaction $i_{t}$ can serve as a training target with prefix history $H_{u}^{(t)}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-1})$ . Let $\mathbf{s}_{u}^{(t)}$ denote the available context formed from this prefix. The training examples are drawn from
 | (1) | | $\mathcal{D}\subseteq\bigcup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\bigl\{(\mathbf{s}_{u}^{(t)},i_{t})\mid 2\leq t<n\bigr\}.$ | 
## 3. R2QRec
## 3.1. Query Generation and Retrieval
R2QRec learns a query generator $Q_{\phi}(q\mid s_{u})$ from item retrieval outcomes while keeping the retriever $R(i|q)$ fixed (Figure 2). The generator expresses the user context as natural-language retrieval queries. We denote a generated query set by $q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{K})$ , where each $q_{j}$ defines a retrieval request. At inference, we infer $K$ queries and combine their retrieval results by rank fusion. The query budget $K$ can be varied at inference without changing the learned parameters.
The frozen retriever $R$ maps each query to a ranked list over $\mathcal{I}\setminus H_{u}$ . Let $\mathrm{rank}_{R}(i\mid q_{j})$ denote the item’s rank in this list. Queries and item documents are encoded in the same semantic embedding space, and item embeddings can be cached. While the generator learns to express preferences in free-form text, it retrieves relevant items in this embedding space. Finally, Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) (Cormack et al., 2009) combines the $K$ retrieval lists. We return the top- $N$ items in the fused ranking as recommendations. Let $\rho_{F}(i\mid q)$ denote the rank of item $i$ after applying this fusion to the query set $q$ . We use the same retrieval pool, history masking, fusion settings, and tie handling when evaluating training query sets.
## 3.2. Training Objective
The two stages connect different levels of item feedback. Item-verified supervision selects admissible retrieval expressions for initial training; fusion-aware refinement evaluates the recommendation list produced by newly generated query sets. We evaluate the latter through the observed next item’s rank and regularize the generator toward a reference policy trained on verified queries. Let $Q_{\phi^{(0)}}$ denote the fixed reference obtained by supervised fine tuning (SFT) on retrieval-verified queries.
For a training pair $(\mathbf{s}^{(t)},i_{t})$ , the regularized objective is
 | | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\phi\mid\mathbf{s}^{(t)},i_{t})$ | $\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q\sim Q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s}^{(t)})}[\ell_{R}(i_{t},q)]}_{\text{retrieval rank loss}}$ | 
 | (2) | | | $\displaystyle\quad+\beta\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(Q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s}^{(t)})\,\|\,Q_{\phi^{(0)}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s}^{(t)})\right)}_{\text{reference regularization}},$ | 
Here, $\ell_{R}(i_{t},q)=\log\!\bigl(1+\rho_{F}(i_{t}\mid q)\bigr)$ . This rank loss evaluates the target position in the combined recommendation list produced by the query set. It requires retrieval ranks and no probability normalization over items. The KL term penalizes deviation from the SFT reference.
The objective measures recovery of an observed interaction through retrieval, allowing different query phrasings to receive feedback. We learn the generator in two stages: SFT constructs the initialization and reference policy, followed by group-relative policy updates using retrieval ranks. The latter use a policy surrogate for discrete generation and ranking, with a log-probability-based estimate of the regularization term.
## 3.3. Item-Verified Supervision
Interaction logs specify which item was selected but do not provide the query that would retrieve it. We construct supervision by generating candidate queries and verifying them with $R$ . The observed item provides a criterion for evaluating candidate expressions without fixing their wording. For each training pair $(\mathbf{s}_{u}^{(t)},i_{t})$ , the base LLM generates a candidate set $\mathcal{Q}_{u}^{(t)}$ of $M$ query strings. We retain queries that retrieve $i_{t}$ within the acceptance threshold $\tau$ :
 | (3) | | $\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{u}^{(t)}=\bigl\{q_{j}\in\mathcal{Q}_{u}^{(t)}\mid\mathrm{rank}_{R}(i_{t}\mid q_{j})\leq\tau\bigr\}.$ | 
Only pairs with at least one accepted query enter the SFT set. We use the retained queries to construct training responses $v$ , yielding a dataset $\hat{\mathcal{D}}=\{(\mathbf{s},v)\}$ . The acceptance threshold $\tau$ selects initial supervision independently of the recommendation cutoff $N$ . The target item is used by the retrieval verifier and is not appended to the generator’s input. Consequently, a query can become a training target through its retrieval behavior even when it differs from the observed item’s title. For a training response $v$ with tokens $v_{\ell}$ , we minimize
 | (4) | | $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\phi)=-\sum_{(\mathbf{s},v)\in\hat{\mathcal{D}}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{|v|}\log Q_{\phi}(v_{\ell}\mid\mathbf{s},v_{<\ell}).$ | 
We update LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) and keep the LLM backbone and retriever frozen. The resulting checkpoint $Q_{\phi^{(0)}}$ initializes reinforcement learning and serves as its reference policy.
## 3.4. Retrieval Rank Feedback
SFT imitates accepted queries, while reinforcement learning compares the retrieval outcomes of newly generated query sets. For each training context $\mathbf{s}$ and target $i_{t}$ , we generate $G$ candidate query sets $q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(G)}$ from $Q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s})$ . Each set contains $K$ queries. We execute the queries, fuse their retrieval lists with RRF, and compute the reward from the resulting rank of $i_{t}$ :
 | (5) | | $r^{(g)}=-\log\!\left(1+\rho_{F}(i_{t}\mid q^{(g)})\right).$ | 
The reward evaluates the combined recommendation list through the same fusion operator used to produce recommendations. Log-rank feedback distinguishes fused target ranks beyond the recommendation cutoff, where cutoff-based hit rewards are tied.†† An earlier Books study recorded approximately $88\%$ zero reward-gradient steps with binary hit feedback, compared with $6.4\%$ with log-rank feedback.
On the same rollouts, strictly monotonic rank feedback preserves at least as many groups with nonzero centered advantages as binary hit feedback; Appendix C.4 establishes this comparison.
## 3.5. Fusion-Aware Refinement
The counts $K$ and $G$ have distinct roles. $K$ controls how many retrieval requests a single response contains; $G$ controls how many alternative responses are compared for one context. With the default $K=5$ and $G=4$ , we request four responses of five queries and assign one reward to each response.
We use GRPO’s within-group reward normalization (Shao et al., 2024) to compute the group relative advantage:
 | (6) | | $\hat{A}^{(g)}=\frac{r^{(g)}-\bar{r}}{\sigma_{r}+\epsilon},$ | 
where $\bar{r}$ and $\sigma_{r}$ are the mean and standard deviation of the $G$ rewards for the same context, and $\epsilon>0$ stabilizes the denominator. The group mean provides a context-specific baseline: a positive $\hat{A}^{(g)}$ identifies a response with an above-average retrieval reward, while a negative value identifies one with a below-average reward. Dividing by the group standard deviation normalizes the scale of these relative comparisons. Every query in a response shares the same set-level advantage. If all rewards in a group coincide, the centered advantages are zero. In particular, $G=1$ removes the relative reward signal. The policy loss combines the advantage signal with a KL penalty:
 | | $\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RL}}(\phi)$ | $\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\hat{A}^{(g)}\frac{\log Q_{\phi}(q^{(g)}\mid\mathbf{s})}{T_{g}}$ | 
 | (7) | | | $\displaystyle\quad+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(Q_{\phi}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s})\,\|\,Q_{\phi^{(0)}}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s})\right).$ | 
The first term is the policy learning term, averaged over the $G$ generated responses. Here $\log Q_{\phi}(q^{(g)}\mid\mathbf{s})$ is the sum of token log probabilities for the generated response, and $T_{g}$ is its token count. Their ratio uses the mean token log probability so that the term does not grow simply by summing over a longer response. With advantages held fixed during the update, minimizing the negative advantage-weighted term encourages higher probability for above-average responses and lower probability for below-average ones. The resulting advantage updates the complete response and is determined by its fused target rank.
The second term regularizes the current policy toward the frozen SFT policy $Q_{\phi^{(0)}}$ . The direction $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(Q_{\phi}\|Q_{\phi^{(0)}})$ penalizes assigning probability to outputs that the reference considers unlikely. The coefficient $\beta$ controls how strongly this reference constrains rank-driven updates: a larger value favors staying closer to the retrieval-verified initialization. We compute the regularization term from response log probabilities under the current policy and the frozen reference. We use one gradient update per rollout group with gradient norm clipping, without reusing rollouts across multiple optimization epochs.
## 3.6. From Item Feedback to Recommendation Utility
The design separates three questions: what an observed item can supervise, how query combinations affect item competition, and which recommendation depths the loss emphasizes. An item label specifies a retrieval target without prescribing a unique expression. Item-verified supervision uses it to define admissible queries; Appendix C.1 formalizes this selection by conditioning on acceptance.
Admissibility alone does not characterize the outcome of combining queries. Lemma C.1 gives two sets of retrieval lists with identical target-rank vectors but different fused target ranks. The difference arises from competing items across the lists: even the full vector of individual target ranks, and therefore its best or mean, cannot determine the fused outcome in general. Fusion-aware refinement observes this combined ranking when comparing generated sets.
Having specified the feedback level, the log-rank objective determines its weighting across recommendation depths.
## Proposition 3.1 (Weighted cutoff-risk decomposition).
Fix an event with $L\geq 1$ eligible items and one target. Let $Z\in\{1,\ldots,L\}$ be its fused rank under any distribution over generated query sets, and let $h_{n}=\Pr(Z\leq n)$ . Then
 | (8) | | $\mathbb{E}[\log(1+Z)]=\log 2+\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}w_{n}(1-h_{n}),\quad w_{n}=\log\frac{n+2}{n+1}.$ | 
## Proof sketch.
Expand $\log(1+Z)-\log 2$ into adjacent log increments and take expectations. Appendix C.3 gives the full derivation. ∎
With one target per event, $h_{n}$ is the expected Recall@ $n$ . Thus, the retrieval loss is a depth-weighted recommendation risk: it weights missed-target events across cutoffs, with positive weights that decrease toward the tail. An equal one-position improvement receives more reward near the head, while improvements beyond a particular cutoff still provide feedback. The identity explains the objective’s weighting across depths; it does not imply improvement at every individual cutoff.

## 4.2. Implementation Details
We implement R2QRec in PyTorch and train on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU with 80 GB memory. We search the SFT acceptance threshold $\tau$ from 50 to 1,000, using the same training-only candidate pool and SFT settings for each threshold. Each domain’s threshold is selected by validation NDCG@10 after SFT and fixed before policy refinement.
Both training stages use LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) on the attention query, key, value, and output projections $(W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V},W_{O})$ . We set the adapter rank to $r=16$ and the scaling parameter to $\alpha=32$ . The backbone and retrieval encoder remain frozen. SFT runs for three epochs with learning rate $2\times 10^{-4}$ , per-device batch size 2, gradient accumulation over eight batches, and a 3% warmup ratio. The SFT checkpoint remains fixed as the KL reference.
We search $G$ from 1 to 32, $\beta$ from 0 to 1, and inference query count $K$ from 1 to 20. For all baseline models, we carefully tune hyperparameters on the validation sets and report test performance using the best validation-selected configuration. Results are averaged over five random runs. Reported improvements over the best-performing baselines are statistically significant ( $p<0.05$ ), assessed using a two-sided $t$ -test.
## 4.3. Baselines
We compare two groups of baselines. Discriminative and generative recommendation. SASRec (Kang and McAuley, 2018) and BERT4Rec (Sun et al., 2019) model interaction sequences; LightGCN (He et al., 2020) learns from the interaction graph; UniSRec (Hou et al., 2022) incorporates item text for transferable sequential recommendation. TIGER (Rajput et al., 2023) and LETTER (Wang et al., 2024) generate item identifiers. Query-based recommendation. BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) and InputRetrieval perform lexical and dense retrieval, respectively, from the same user context and item documents as R2QRec, without an LLM. BIGRec (Bao et al., 2025) generates target-item text and grounds it to the catalog. GPT4Rec (Li et al., 2023b) trains a query generator using item text; the concurrent QueryAgent-R1 (Sun et al., 2026a) uses an agentic search procedure to refine e-commerce query recommendations. QueRec (Han et al., 2025) combines personalized-query retrieval with collaborative scores through adaptive reranking, without LLM fine tuning. Trainable text-generation baselines use their respective training objectives. GPT4Rec and BIGRec use the same Qwen2.5-7B backbone, frozen MiniLM retriever, $K=5$ query budget, and rank-fusion procedure as R2QRec. The zero-shot generator uses the same backbone, prompt, and retrieval pipeline as R2QRec, without task training.
## 4.4. Results on Public Datasets (RQ1)
Table 2 reports recommendation results over the full item set. We make four observations.
• 
Leading results across domains. R2QRec achieves the best results among the evaluated methods in 23 of the 24 dataset–metric comparisons. Its relative NDCG@10 gains over the strongest baseline are $68.7\%$ , $3.5\%$ , and $42.1\%$ on Yelp, MovieTV, and Books, respectively.
• 
Strong performance on short recommendation lists. On MovieTV, R2QRec improves Recall@5 and Recall@10 by $11.9\%$ and $17.3\%$ over the strongest baseline. BERT4Rec leads at Recall@50 ( $13.95\%$ versus $13.67\%$ ), where R2QRec ranks second, while R2QRec leads at every NDCG cutoff. This pattern highlights its effectiveness at placing relevant items near the top of the recommendation list.
• 
Effectiveness of generated retrieval queries. Interestingly, LLM-based query recommenders provide the strongest baseline in 21 of the 24 comparisons. GPT4Rec leads the baselines on Yelp, while BIGRec and QueryAgent-R1 provide the strongest results on Books. ## 5. Related Work
## 5.1. Query Learning for Recommendation
Query-based recommendation connects language generation with item retrieval, with the closest approaches differing in how they train or construct the generated text. GPT4Rec fine-tunes a language model to predict target item titles, then uses the generated text with BM25 to retrieve recommendations (Li et al., 2023b). Its generator learns target-title likelihood; retrieval ranks do not supervise its parameter updates. BIGRec similarly uses target item text for supervised adaptation before grounding generated text to actual items (Bao et al., 2025).
Rather than adapting the generator to target text, QueRec (Han et al., 2025) generates enriched item descriptions and personalized queries through prompting without LLM fine tuning, and integrates semantic retrieval with collaborative signals. This approach uses an LLM to express preferences, but does not learn its query generation policy from the retrieval outcomes. Rec-R1 couples LLM-generated text with fixed recommendation models (Lin et al., 2025). Concurrent QueryAgent-R1 combines memory-based user profiling with a search agent for proactive e-commerce query recommendation (Sun et al., 2026a). The focus of R2QRec is how item-level observations supervise an intermediate query interface. Retrieval verification converts those observations into query targets without prescribing their wording, while policy refinement evaluates generated sets at the fused ranking used for recommendation. This connects supervision construction and recommendation-level outcome optimization through a fixed retrieval system.
## 5.2. Other Recommendation Approaches
Discriminative recommenders score items through learned representations, with sequential models deriving user representations from interaction histories. GRU4Rec uses recurrent networks (Hidasi et al., 2016), SASRec applies causal self attention (Kang and McAuley, 2018), and BERT4Rec uses masked item prediction (Sun et al., 2019). LightGCN captures collaborative structure through graph propagation (He et al., 2020), while UniSRec uses item text to support transfer across domains (Hou et al., 2022). RecFormer extends this approach to textual item sequences (Li et al., 2023a). Generative recommenders instead predict item identifiers or text. P5 casts recommendation tasks as text generation (Geng et al., 2022); TIGER generates semantic IDs obtained through residual quantization (Rajput et al., 2023); and OneRec develops generative recommendation at industrial scale (Deng et al., 2025). LETTER learns item tokenization with semantic and collaborative regularization (Wang et al., 2024). R2QRec generates retrieval requests that can each match multiple items.
Tags provide another semantic connection between users and items (Xu et al., 2011; Yu et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). SemRec combines structural and semantic relationships in social tagging data for item recommendation (Xu et al., 2011). R2QRec instead learns to compose language requests, with observed-item retrieval outcomes supervising their wording.
Natural-language interfaces broaden how users interact with recommenders (Zhang et al., 2025). Agentic approaches further introduce memory, tool use, and interaction between agents (Chen et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Tao et al., 2026; Kang et al., 2026). The candidate-ranking protocols considered here provide a small item set to the LLM, allowing direct reasoning about candidate content. R2QRec instead generates retrieval queries without candidate enumeration in its prompt, and ranks the supplied candidates by retrieval when evaluated in this setting.
## 6. Conclusion
We presented R2QRec, a framework for learning recommendation queries from item interactions through a fixed retrieval system. Its design separates two learning decisions: retrieval verification identifies useful expressions to imitate, and policy refinement evaluates newly generated query sets through their combined item ranking. The same retrieval interface supports both stages, while only the query generator is updated. Controlled comparisons show the benefit of retrieval-verified supervision over title imitation and the further gain from policy refinement. The resulting query interface supports recommendation over the full item set without requiring query annotations or a learned reward model.
Future work can adapt the query budget to user context and examine alternative fusion functions within query learning. Another direction is to study query refresh policies for changing user interests and item collections. The separation between query generation and retrieval also supports exploring additional retrieval backends. Broader questions of verifier reliability when LLMs arbitrate between prior knowledge and retrieved evidence are also relevant to retrieval-augmented systems more generally (Sun et al., 2026b).

## C.1. Item-Supervised Query Verification
Let $P_{0}(x\mid\mathbf{s})$ be the base proposal distribution over individual query strings, and define
 | (9) | | $\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{s},i_{t}}=\{x:\mathrm{rank}_{R}(i_{t}\mid x)\leq\tau\}.$ | 
Write $a=P_{0}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{s},i_{t}}\mid\mathbf{s})>0$ . Ideal rejection sampling yields the conditional distribution
 | (10) | | $P_{\mathrm{acc}}(x\mid\mathbf{s},i_{t})=\frac{P_{0}(x\mid\mathbf{s})\mathbf{1}[x\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{s},i_{t}}]}{a}.$ | 
For any distribution $P$ supported on accepted queries with finite $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|P_{0})$ ,
 | (11) | | $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|P_{0})=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|P_{\mathrm{acc}})-\log a.$ | 
On the accepted support, $P_{0}(x\mid\mathbf{s})=aP_{\mathrm{acc}}(x\mid\mathbf{s},i_{t})$ . Substitution into $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\log(P(x)/P_{0}(x))$ gives Eq. 11. The nonnegativity of KL then identifies $P_{\mathrm{acc}}$ as the closest such distribution to $P_{0}$ in $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|P_{0})$ .
This standard conditional-distribution identity makes the role of the item label explicit: it defines which expressions are admissible, while their relative proposal probabilities are retained within the accepted set. Our SFT uses finite accepted samples assembled into responses, so the identity describes query selection rather than an exact solution for the trained response distribution. The identity assumes a nonzero acceptance probability.
## C.2. Query-Level Ranks and Fused Utility
Consider RRF (Cormack et al., 2009) over complete ranked lists, with a fixed constant $c>0$ and score
 | (12) | | $S_{q}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{c+\mathrm{rank}_{R}(i\mid q_{j})}.$ | 
## Lemma C.1 (Individual ranks do not determine fused rank).
For any $c>0$ , the target-rank vector alone does not determine the target’s RRF rank over general collections of complete ranked lists.
## Proof.
For target $t$ and competing items $a,b$ , consider two lists per set:
 | Set | List 1 | List 2 | Target ranks | Fused rank
 | $A$ | $(a,t,b)$ | $(a,t,b)$ | $(2,2)$ | $2$ 
 | $B$ | $(a,t,b)$ | $(b,t,a)$ | $(2,2)$ | $3$ 
In $A$ , item $a$ remains above $t$ and item $b$ remains below it after fusion. In $B$ , each competitor obtains one first and one third position, giving the score difference
 | | $\displaystyle S_{B}(a)-S_{B}(t)$ | $\displaystyle=S_{B}(b)-S_{B}(t)$ | 
 | | | $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c+1}+\frac{1}{c+3}-\frac{2}{c+2}$ | 
 | (13) | | | $\displaystyle=\frac{2}{(c+1)(c+2)(c+3)}>0.$ | 
Both competitors therefore outrank $t$ despite unchanged target ranks in the individual lists. Thus, even the full target-rank vector, including its best and average ranks, is insufficient to determine fused utility. ∎
The construction does not imply that greater query diversity necessarily improves recommendations: useful combinations depend on their effects on both the target and competing items.
## C.3. Weighted Top- $N$ Miss Risk
## Proof of Proposition 3.1.
For any realized rank $z\in\{1,\ldots,L\}$ ,
 | | $\displaystyle\log(1+z)-\log 2$ | $\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{z-1}\bigl[\log(n+2)-\log(n+1)\bigr]$ | 
 | (14) | | | $\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{L-1}\log\frac{n+2}{n+1}\,\mathbf{1}[z>n].$ | 
Taking expectations proves Eq. 8, since $\Pr(Z>n)=1-h_{n}$ ; the sum is empty when $L=1$ . ∎
For events with different eligible-pool sizes $L_{e}$ , use the common upper bound $L_{\max}=\max_{e}L_{e}$ and set $h_{e,n}=1$ for $n\geq L_{e}$ . The added terms are zero, so averaging the identity across events makes the averaged $h_{e,n}$ the expected Recall@ $n$ in the single-target setting. Each weight $w_{n}$ is positive and decreases with $n$ . Equivalently, improving a rank from $r+1$ to $r$ increases reward by $\log((r+2)/(r+1))$ , giving larger per-position gains near the head.
The expansion characterizes the loss as a weighted combination of missed-target risks, rather than as the loss at any single cutoff. A smaller expected log-rank therefore need not improve every individual Recall@ $N$ or NDCG@ $N$ . For truncated rank measurements with a finite missing-target sentinel, the same algebra applies to that measured rank variable; its interpretation is correspondingly limited to the recorded depths.
## C.4. Resolution of Group-Relative Feedback
## Lemma C.2 (Resolution of centered advantages).
For a fixed group of $G\geq 1$ target ranks $z_{1},\ldots,z_{G}$ and a cutoff $N$ , let $b_{g}=\mathbf{1}[z_{g}\leq N]$ and $r_{g}=-\log(1+z_{g})$ . If the centered binary-reward vector is nonzero, then the centered rank-reward vector is nonzero.
## Proof.
A centered vector is nonzero exactly when the underlying rewards are nonconstant. Nonconstant binary rewards imply distinct ranks, which yield distinct log-rank rewards by strict monotonicity. ∎
The converse need not hold: different ranks on the same side of $N$ have equal binary rewards. Thus, on any fixed collection of rollout groups, rank feedback preserves at least as many groups with nonzero centered advantages as binary hit feedback, without an independence assumption.
For a probabilistic expression, additionally suppose that the $G$ ranks are conditionally independent draws from $p_{z}=\Pr(Z=z)$ for the fixed event. Let $p_{\mathrm{hit}}=\sum_{z\leq N}p_{z}$ . The probabilities of a constant-reward group are
 | | $\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{rank}}$ | $\displaystyle=\sum_{z=1}^{L}p_{z}^{G},$ | 
 | (15) | | $\displaystyle P_{\mathrm{hit}}$ | $\displaystyle=p_{\mathrm{hit}}^{G}+(1-p_{\mathrm{hit}})^{G}\geq P_{\mathrm{rank}}.$ | 
The inequality follows by grouping the rank probabilities into the hit and miss sets and expanding their $G$ th powers. It holds with equality for $G=1$ ; for $G>1$ , it is strict if either set contains at least two ranks of positive probability.
Nonzero centered advantages need not yield a nonzero parameter gradient: score-function terms may cancel, and KL contributes separately. Any strictly monotonic rank reward shares this resolution property, while Appendix C.3 specifies log-rank’s weighting.
