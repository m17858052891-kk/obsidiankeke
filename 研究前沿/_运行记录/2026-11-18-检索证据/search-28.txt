## Abstract

In collaborative filtering recommender systems user’s preferences are expressed as ratings for items, and each additional rating extends the knowledge of the system and affects the system’s recommendation accuracy. In general, the more ratings are elicited from the users, the more effective the recommendations are. However, the usefulness of each rating may vary significantly, i.e., different ratings may bring a different amount and type of information about the user’s tastes. Hence, specific techniques, which are defined as “active learning strategies”, can be used to selectively choose the items to be presented to the user for rating. In fact, an active learning strategy identifies and adopts criteria for obtaining data that better reflects users’ preferences and enables to generate better recommendations.

So far, a variety of active learning strategies have been proposed in the literature. In this article, we survey recent strategies by grouping them with respect to two distinct dimensions: personalization, i.e., whether the system selected items are different for different users or not, and, hybridization, i.e., whether active learning is guided by a single criterion (heuristic) or by multiple criteria. In addition, we present a comprehensive overview of the evaluation methods and metrics that have been employed by the research community in order to test active learning strategies for collaborative filtering. Finally, we compare the surveyed strategies and provide guidelines for their usage in recommender systems.

## 5 Conclusions

This paper addressed the growing need for rigorous and reproducible practices in EA. While theoretical algorithm analysis offers invaluable insights, it often relies on simplified and sometimes outdated computational models that may not reflect real-world performance. In contrast, EA provides empirical evidence of algorithm behavior on actual input data and real hardware. However, the lack of standardized environments for conducting and verifying such experiments poses a significant barrier to transparency and collaboration.

To mitigate this, we proposed a formal model for algorithm evaluation, which introduces clearly defined entities such as inputs, outputs, test cases, test sets, algorithms, input parameters, and evaluation indicators. This model supports an automated experimentation pipeline encompassing test case generation, algorithm execution, output validation, and results presentation. An important role in this model is played by the Test Set Iterator and the Experiment Crawler processes. The former systematically traverses predefined sets of generating parameters to produce corresponding test cases, while the latter dynamically creates and executes test cases based on parameter space exploration and intermediate results.

We also presented ALGator, an open-source system that implements this model and enables collaborative development and evaluation of algorithmic solutions. ALGator facilitates public sharing of experimental setups and results, thereby promoting verifiability, repeatability, and extensibility in algorithm evaluation. Researchers can contribute new algorithms, test cases, or performance indicators, building upon existing experiments without redundancy.

A case study on array-sorting algorithms demonstrated the practical utility of our approach. Specifically, we revisited performance claims about Yaroslavskiy's Quicksort compared to Hoare's implementation. While earlier claims suggested Yaroslavskiy's superiority in general, our experiments—conducted on both randomly ordered arrays and arrays with varying repetition ratios—showed more nuanced results. Notably, Hoare's version often outperformed Yaroslavskiy's on random input without duplicates, while the latter excelled when duplicate values were prevalent. These findings align in trend, but not in magnitude, with previously published results, underscoring the importance of experiment design and environment reproducibility.

In conclusion, the combination of a formal experimental model and its practical implementation in ALGator represents a step forward in standardizing and democratizing EA. Future work will involve expanding the range of implemented problem domains, refining the model to cover more advanced experimental scenarios, and fostering a broader community of users contributing to open, verifiable algorithmic research.

# Self-Training

Pattern

Iteratively use selected model predictions as pseudo-labels for unlabeled data, retrain the model, and regenerate those labels with the updated model.

Also known as: self-training loop · pseudo-labeling loop · iterative pseudo-labeling · pseudo-label refinement · teacher-student self-training

## Problem

Training may start with only a small labeled dataset while a much larger pool of unlabeled examples is available for the same task. Expanding the labeled set manually can be too slow or expensive, leaving most of the available data unused.

A model trained on the available labels can generate labels for this unlabeled pool, but prediction quality varies across examples. The model is usually most reliable on cases it already handles confidently, while many of its remaining errors occur on cases where its predictions are unreliable. Adding those incorrect model-generated labels to training can reinforce existing errors instead of correcting them.

## Core Idea

Use the current model to assign preliminary labels, called pseudo-labels, to unlabeled examples that meet a reliability criterion. --------------------------------------------------------------------------------
