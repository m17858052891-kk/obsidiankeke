                                                    Abstract

          Existing multi-task active learning (MTL-AL) methods predominantly rely on
          the linear scalarization of heterogeneous objectives, such as uncertainty, diversity,
          and gradient conflict. We argue that this entangled formulation is fundamentally
          limited, as linear combination fails to capture Pareto-optimal trade-offs under con-
          flicting objectives. To address this limitation, we propose a paradigm shift from
          combinationtodeconstructionand introduce a principled framework that rethinks
          MTL-AL along three orthogonal dimensions. First, we identify theConflict Para-
          dox: although the optimization literature treats gradient conflict as interference to
          be suppressed, carrying that intuition into the acquisition score is counterproductive
          — penalizing conflict when ranking candidates is measurably worse than not scoring
          it at all.  We therefore decouple the two concerns: the acquisition function does
          not score conflict, and the interference it causes is resolved separately by gradient
          surgery during training. Second, we introduceOrthogonal Decomposition, sepa-
          rating the acquisition objective into two distinct axes:InformativenessandExplo-
          ration. This two-stage process, consisting of informativeness filtering followed by
          coverage maximization, prevents “diversity dilution” by excluding uninformative
          outliers before enforcing diversity.  Third, we proposeCross-Task Mutual Infor-
          mation (CTMI), a covariance-based statistic of cross-task dependency computed
          from MC-dropout task entropies. Extensive experiments on COCO, Cityscapes,
          and NYUv2 — including comparisons against recent multi-task active learning
          and data-selection baselines — demonstrate consistent improvements over state-
          of-the-art MTL-AL methods, highlighting that structural rethinking of acquisition
          yields larger gains than incremental heuristic design. Our code is publicly available
          at:github.com/Juho-Jung/multitask_active_learning_official

1    Introduction

Multi-task learning (MTL) has emerged as a fundamental paradigm for building efficient and gen-
eralizable representations by leveraging shared structures across related tasks [5, 32]. However, its
success highly depends on the availability of densely annotated data for every task, resulting in pro-
hibitive annotation costs as the number and complexity of tasks grow [52, 43]. Active Learning (AL)
offers an approach to mitigate this burden by selectively querying the most valuable samples [35]. Yet,
extending AL to MTL settings introduces a fundamental challenge: the notion of “informativeness”
becomes inherently multi-faceted, requiring the joint consideration of uncertainty, diversity, and
inter-task relationships [47].

The Fundamental Problem: Linear Combination.Most existing MTL active learning methods
address  this  challenge  by  formulating  sample  selection  as  a  multi-objective  optimization  prob-
lem solved via linear scalarization [47, 17, 15, 16, 29, 31, 6].  Representative approaches such as
BADGE [2] and BatchBALD [18] combine uncertainty and diversity through weighted summation. In
multi-task settings, this strategy is commonly extended by aggregating task-wise uncertainties [41, 50]
or incorporating penalties for gradient conflict [51, 7]. We argue this reliance on linear combination
reflects a fundamental geometric limitation rather than a mere modeling choice. Since the objectives
are incommensurable and measured in different units (e.g., entropy vs. feature distance vs. gradient
cosine), linear scalarization fails to discover non-convex regions of the Pareto frontier [33, 23], often
leading to sub-optimal trade-offs driven by arbitrary weighting.  This limitation manifests in two
failure modes: “Diversity Dilution,” where outliers dominate due to extreme distance scores [               34], and
the “Conflict Paradox,” where samples informative for inter-task trade-offs are prematurely discarded.

Deconstruction as a paradigm.Rather than seeking better weighting schemes, we rethink the
problem structure via two principles: (i)Decoupling Selection from Optimization— keep gradient
conflict out of the acquisition score entirely, and resolve the interference it causes at training time
via gradient surgery, instead of letting an optimization-time concern filter which samples are ever
labelled; (ii)Orthogonal Decomposition— decompose acquisition into two sequential, conceptually
orthogonal axes (InformativenessandExploration) and apply them hierarchically so that diversity is
enforced only within a subspace of genuinely informative candidates.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
 •  We identify theConflict Paradox:  penalizing gradient conflict inside the acquisition score is
   measurably worse than not scoring conflict at all, and we introduce a principled decoupling that
   leaves conflict to the optimizer rather than to the selector.
 •  We introduceOrthogonal Decomposition, a two-stage selection strategy that first filters candidates
   by informativeness and subsequently maximizes coverage within the retained set, preventing
   diversity dilution.
 •  We proposeCross-Task Mutual Information (CTMI), a forward-pass-only statistic of cross-task
   dependency, and show that any non-zero cross-task covariance in our architecture is structurally
   attributable to the shared representation; we validate CTMI as an acquisition signal by ablation
   rather than by a distributional bound.
 •  We  demonstrate  consistent  improvements  on  three  benchmarks  —  COCO  (natural  images),
   Cityscapes (autonomous driving) and NYUv2 (indoor scenes) — reaching +10.0%         over Random
   and+2.4%to+3.0%over prior active-learning methods under our main protocol (Table 2), and
   the highest paired gain against five recent multi-stage selection methods under a separate unified
   multi-seed protocol (Table 3).

2    Related Work

Active  learning  and  the  linear-combination  trap.Deep  AL  is  commonly  categorized  into
uncertainty-based methods (entropy [37, 12], margin [30], MC-Dropout [11], deep ensembles [21]),
diversity-based methods (CoreSet [34], discriminative coverage [14, 41]), and hybrids that linearly
combine the two:  BADGE [2] combines gradient magnitude with k-means++ clustering, Batch-
BALD [18] maximizes batch-level MI, and VAAL [41]/Learning Loss [50] learn auxiliary scalar
predictors. As we show in Section 6 and Appendix G.2, this linear-combination paradigm suffers
from“Diversity Dilution”— uninformative outliers with extreme distance scores dominate selection.

Multi-task learning, gradient conflict, and MTL active learning.   MTL suffers from gradient
conflict between tasks,  and every established remedy acts atoptimizationtime:  task balancing
(GradNorm [7], MGDA [33]) and gradient surgery (PCGrad [51], CAGrad [24]). Cross-task conflict
has, to our knowledge, never been used as anacquisitionsignal: multi-task AL scores candidates
by aggregated per-task uncertainty [29] or by extending single-task heuristics [36, 38, 31], leaving
the selection stage blind to inter-task structure. We call the resulting situation theConflict Paradox:
when a conflict termisintroduced into the acquisition score,  penalizing it — the reading that
the optimization literature invites — is measurably worse than omitting it altogether (Table 4).
The closest recent methods, Hekimoglu et al. [16, 17] (PAD-Net inconsistency + diversity) and
PartAL [10] (per-task partial labeling), depend on specific task pairings or architectural components;

Two-stage selection is established practice.Filtering a candidate pool by an informativeness score
and then selecting for coverage within the filtered set is a well-established template.  FASS [45]
filters by uncertainty before submodular subset selection; Cluster-Margin [8] margin-filters before
cluster-diverse sampling; PPAL [49] applies difficulty-calibrated uncertainty sampling followed by
category-conditioned diversity; D2-Pruning [25] and SES [48] balance difficulty against diversity
through graph message passing and structural entropy respectively; and related submodular and
coverage-based formulations are surveyed in [34, 2].  Our contribution is the multi-task-specific
quantity that drives its first stage — a cross-task dependency statistic computed from the shared
encoder — together with the analysis motivating it. Section 6 isolates exactly this by running the
same two-stage template with single-task signals under one identical multi-seed protocol.

3    The Conflict Paradox

Existing multi-task active learning methods penalize gradient conflict, treating it as optimization
noise [24, 51, 39, 7].  As illustrated in Figure 1, this assumption induces a systemic bias toward
the “aligned” region (blue area), where samples follow the model’s current optimization trajectory.
However, our retrospective analysis shows that after early training, samples yielding the largest
joint-loss reduction concentrate in high-conflict regions (red area). Penalizing conflict thus discards
high-gain samples. Motivated by this, we propose theDecoupling Principle, which explicitly targets
this informative yet neglected region.

3.2    The Decoupling Principle

Based on Proposition 1, we resolve the tension between selection and optimization by acting on each
separately: atselectionthe acquisition score contains no conflict term at all, so conflicting samples
are neither sought out nor filtered away; atoptimizationwe apply gradient surgery (PCGrad [                     51]) to
project conflicting gradients onto the normal plane and stabilize updates without discarding the data.

4    Orthogonal Decomposition of Acquisition

We proposeOrthogonal Decomposition Selection (ODS), a framework that departs from linear
scalarization by decomposing acquisition into two structurally distinct axes:Informativeness (   I)
andExploration (    E).  ODS operates as a three-phase decoupled pipeline (Figure 2):(1) Scoring
— compute per-task predictive uncertainty and its cross-task structure under MC-dropout;(2) Two-
Stage Selection— filter the pool to the high-          I candidates, then maximize coverage within them;(3)
Decoupled Optimization— train the selected batch under PCGrad [                     51], which resolves whatever
gradient conflict the batch happens to contain.

5    Cross-Task Mutual Information (CTMI)

tainty alone is insufficient for this purpose.  Two samples with the same mean entropy can have
entirely different annotation value. If all tasks are uncertain due to a single shared bottleneck, their
labels yield redundant supervisory signals. If they are uncertain for independent reasons, a single an-
notation resolves multiple distinct ambiguities. We therefore complement the uncertaintymagnitude
(Hardness) with a statistic of its cross-taskstructure, and prioritize samples whose task uncertainties
arenotexplained by a single shared cause.

                                      5.3    On Combining Hardness and CTMI

                                      Equation (5) is a weighted sum, which may appear to sit uneasily beside our critique of linear
                                      scalarization. The two operate at different levels. Our Diversity-Dilution argument concerns sums
                                      acrossheterogeneous axes, namely model uncertainty against feature-space coverage, where one
                                      axis is measured in entropy and the other in feature distance, so that a single extreme coordinate
                                      can dominate and induce a non-Pareto trade-off. Hardness and CTMI are instead two functionals of
                                      thesameMC-dropout predictive distribution, both normalized to            [0,1]     over the same pool and both
                                      monotone in “unresolved by the current model”. Their sum therefore acts as a soft conjunction of
                                      two screening conditions rather than a trade-off between competing objectives: no sample can rank
                                      highly onIwithout being uncertain.
                                      While this weighted sum is ultimately a heuristic, its exact formulation matters little.  Alternative
                                      combinations—such as a rank-sum, z-score sum, or a product—yield statistically indistinguishable
                                      results over three seeds (0.723   –0.731   ).  The acquisition gain therefore originates in the cross-task
                                      signal itself rather than the specific combination rule.

6    Experiments

We validate our framework across three diverse benchmarks:COCO(general scenes),Cityscapes
(autonomous driving), andNYUv2(indoor) to answer three questions:

1.  Performance:Does ODS outperform state-of-the-art linear combination methods in data effi-
    ciency?
2.  Paradox Verification:Does embracing gradient conflict actually improve learning compared to
    avoiding it?
3.Theoretical Validity:Does CTMI serve as a valid proxy for inter-task mutual information?

6.1    Experimental Setup

Datasets.We consider datasets with varying task affinity (additional details are provided in Ap-
pendix B.4.):

• COCO [22]: Object detection, instance segmentation, and image-level classification.
• Cityscapes [9]: Semantic segmentation, depth estimation, and instance segmentation.
• NYUv2 [40]: Indoor semantic segmentation, depth, and surface normal estimation.

Baselines.We compare against representative methods spanning three categories:(1) Uncertainty-
based:Random,Entropy(mean task entropy),Margin, andLearning Loss[                                                     50],(2) Diversity-based:
CoreSet[       34] (feature distance maximization),  and(3) Hybrid / MTL-Extended:BADGE[                                             2],
BatchBALD[        18], andMT-BADGE (Ext.)(our extension of BADGE via gradient concatenation).
Note that we focus on architecture-agnostic and task-general active learning baselines to ensure
fair comparison across diverse multi-task settings. Recent task-specific or architecture-dependent

detailed justification for including specific recent work in Appendix C.1.

Implementation Details & Fairness Control.We use lightweight, non-pretrained architectures
(Appendix B.2) to isolate acquisition effects from pretraining. All methods share the same backbone,
optimizer (PCGrad [51]), and AL protocol (1% seed, 1% budget per round, 30 rounds). For regression
tasks (e.g., depth), uncertainty is the log-variance of MC-Dropout predictions (Appendix B.3).

                               7    Limitations

                               Our analysis has four explicit limitations.  Proposition 1 is a sample-level, local property of the
                               per-sample Jacobian rather than a global Pareto-stationarity claim, making the bridge to AL utility
                               empirical (Table 4). CTMI relies on covariance and is blind to non-linear dependencies; however,
                               distance correlation tracks Pearson within 0.05    on COCO (Appendix D.3), indicating task uncertainty
                               dependence is predominantly linear here. Domains with strongly non-linear coupling may require
                               different statistics.  ODS introduces hyperparameters (m, K, α, γ)    ; while robust (Appendices F.5,
                               F.6), per-domain tuning may help.  Finally, main results use lightweight scratch encoders; a pre-
                               trained ResNet-50 (Appendix E.2) preserves rank ordering but narrows gaps, leaving comprehensive
                               evaluations with modern foundation backbones to future work.

                               8    Conclusion

                               This work replaces the standard linear combination paradigm in multi-task active learning with
                               objective deconstruction. We identify theConflict Paradox, showing that importing the optimizer’s
                               aversion to gradient conflict into the acquisition score degrades accuracy rather than protecting it,
                               and resolve this by decoupling sample selection from optimization. By separating informativeness
                               from exploration, ourOrthogonal Decompositionframework—driven byCross-Task Mutual
                               Information (CTMI)—prevents the diversity dilution that plagues scalarized objectives. Results
                               across COCO, Cityscapes, and NYUv2 show consistent state-of-the-art performance, demonstrating
                               that progress in MTL-AL lies in exploiting objective orthogonality rather than tuning heuristic
                               scalarizations.

G    Qualitative Analysis

G.1    What the Conflict Ablation Shows

The controlled ablation in Table 4 isolates the effect of the acquisition score: holding the optimizer
(AdamW) and scoring pipeline fixed, penalizing gradient conflict lowers the MTL Composite from
0.076    (ignoring conflict) to 0.059    (penalizing it).  This empirically grounds theConflict Paradox:
penalizing conflict actively degrades data selection.
Crucially, this defines what the acquisition score mustomit, not what it must seek.  ODS ranks
candidates exclusively by Hardness and CTMI (Eq. (5)); gradient conflict is absent from the score.
This does not imply that ODS batches are inherently enriched with high-conflict samples relative
to random selection. To understand the selection behavior, readers should refer to the component
and combination-rule ablations (Appendices F.2 and F.3), which isolate the contribution of each term
without inferring a conflict-driven mechanism.

G.2.1    Distributional Analysis: The Outlier Trap
Figure 5 tracks the t-SNE embeddings of selected samples against the unlabeled pool across Rounds
1–8. A stark behavioral divergence emerges:

        •  Redundant Collapse by Baselines.Diversity-driven baselines (CoreSet,BADGE,Batch-

           outliersthat are distinctive but uninformative.

           so sparsely. By allocating the vast majority of its budget to thehigh-density informative
           manifold, ODS strictly balances outlier exploration with the exploitation of semantically
           rich data.

G.2.2    Visual Redundancy vs. Semantic Diversity
Figures 6 and 7 map these distributional failures to image space.ODSconsistently acquires visually
diverse, semantically dense scenes—from cluttered indoor environments to complex street views.
Conversely, baselines exhibit severe redundancy, selecting visually similar samples despite operating
on different acquisition principles. Geometry-based (CoreSet) and information-theoretic (BatchBALD)
methods often converge on identical outliers, a redundancy mirrored in the t-SNE clusters (Figure 5,
red dashed circles).

Failure Mode I: Semantic Sparsity (“Toilet Trap”).In Round 2 (Figure 6), several baselines
fixate on semantically sparse environments.   Highlighted byred boxes, CoreSet, BADGE, and
BatchBALDrepeatedly acquire images of empty bathrooms, whileEntropyfavors featureless kitchen
counters.  These samples are geometrically rare but contain minimal object interactions.  This is
Diversity Dilutionin action: prioritizing distance metrics collapses the selection onto samples with
low semantic content.

Failure Mode II: Texture and Structural Bias.Baselines also over-sample high-frequency tex-
tures and repetitive structures.  In Round 3 (Figure 7),CoreSetdisproportionately selects donuts,
bananas, and pastries, while other baselines redundantly acquire images of fences or grids. While
visually salient, these scenes offer negligible multi-task supervision compared to scenes with complex
object interactions.

G.2.3    Annotation Density and Multi-Task Supervision
Figures 8 and 9 overlay ground-truth annotations, revealing stark density differences. ODS consis-
tently targets scenes with dense bounding boxes and intricate segmentation masks (e.g., cluttered
desks, crowded streets). These multi-instance scenes yield rich, high-frequency gradient signals that
simultaneously improve detection and segmentation. In contrast, baselines frequently select sparse or
empty images (e.g., ocean waves). These samples are ”hard“ only due to uninformative background
uncertainty, yielding minimal information gain per labeled pixel.

G.2.4    Why ODS Avoids Diversity Dilution
Across both feature-space and image-space analyses, ODS systematically bypasses the failure modes
that trap standard baselines.  By filtering out uninformative outliers before maximizing coverage,
ODS consistently acquires semantically complex, non-redundant data—discovering the high-value
samples that existing MTL-AL strategies overlook.

H    Ethics statement
This work investigates acquisition strategies for multi-task learning and collects no new data. All
experiments rely on the public COCO, Cityscapes, and NYUv2 benchmarks under their respective
licenses.   No human-subject research was conducted,  and we handle no personally identifiable
information beyond the anonymized distributions provided by the dataset creators (e.g., blurred
faces and license plates in Cityscapes). Like any active learning method, our acquisition function
concentrates the annotation budget on a specific data subset,  which may amplify existing pool
imbalances. Because our score relies strictly on predictive uncertainty and cross-task dependency
without explicit fairness constraints, deployment in safety-critical settings requires auditing the
selected batches. We anticipate no risks of misuse beyond those inherent to general label-efficient
learning.

I    Reproducibility statement
All code, configuration files, and analysis scripts required to reproduce our results are publicly
available at github.com/Juho-Jung/multitask_active_learning_official. Appendix B.2
specifies the architectures, optimization settings, and MC-dropout configurations. Table 5 lists the
exact (m, K, α, γ)     hyperparameters for all experimental protocols.  Appendix B.4 documents the
dataset splits and seed sets, and Appendix B.1 details the complete selection procedure. Unless noted
otherwise, results are reported across three random seeds (42, 123, 456) as mean ± standard deviation.
While  non-deterministic  GPU  operations  may  cause  minor  per-round  numerical  variances,  the
performance gaps and rank orderings central to our claims are strictly robust and fully reproducible.
